Abstract

Many kinases share highly similar sequences and structures, which makes it difficult to design binders that engage one target kinase strongly without also binding closely related paralogs. Poor selectivity can cause off-target effects, reduce experimental reliability, and limit an inhibitor's usefulness in medicine. We built a computational pipeline — combining BindCraft and ColabFold (AlphaFold2) for structure prediction and mini-protein binder generation with custom Python/bash selectivity-profiling scripts — to design de novo mini-protein binders and evaluate each candidate against 50+ kinases spanning all 8 major kinase families using the ipTM confidence metric. To make this workflow accessible to researchers without a computational background, we also developed a GUI platform that unifies binder design, selectivity visualization, and a shared public binder library behind a simple point-and-click interface. Our next goal is to push the pipeline toward paralog-level selectivity — for example discriminating LATS1 from LATS2 — and to validate the strongest computational hits experimentally with pull-down assays.

Approach

For each target, we predict its structure, generate candidate mini-protein binders, and then profile every candidate's selectivity against a broad kinase panel before inspecting the top hits visually.

Tools

  • BindCraft — de novo mini-protein binder design
  • ColabFold / AlphaFold2 — target structure prediction
  • Kinase selectivity profiler — in-house Python/bash scripts scoring every candidate against the kinase panel via ipTM
  • PyMOL — visual inspection of target folding and binder–target interactions

Experiments

Step 1 — Cross-family selectivity screening

For each target — NDR1/NDR2, LATS1/LATS2, ROCK1/ROCK2, and Map4k4 — every BindCraft-designed candidate was screened against an 8-kinase panel (and, for NDR2, an extended panel of 50+ kinases spanning all 8 major kinase families) to check for on-target vs. off-target binding using the ipTM confidence metric. Step 2 — Toward within-paralog selectivity: LATS1 vs. LATS2

Our longer-term idea is to fuse a binder that engages the shared kinase domain of both paralogs with a second binder that is selective for one paralog's more divergent N-terminus, so the fusion inherits both affinity and selectivity.

Broader Impact & Next Steps

The project benefits researchers and students working in protein engineering, drug discovery, and computational biology by making kinase binder design more accessible, efficient, and user-friendly. The GUI platform lowers the technical barrier of command-line workflows, letting biology-focused collaborators spend their time on biological questions, interpreting results, and designing experiments rather than managing complex computational tools — and the underlying approach can be adapted to any publicly available kinase.

We will continue refining the pipeline's ability to design selective binders, moving from cross-family selectivity (achieved) toward within-paralog selectivity (in progress, e.g. LATS1 vs. LATS2). Once we can design high-confidence binders that discriminate between LATS1 and LATS2, we plan to test them experimentally with pull-down assays, and to fold the paralog-selective workflow into the GUI platform.
